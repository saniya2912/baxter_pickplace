\begin{abstract}
Human--robot collaboration requires robots to understand high-level goals while performing reliable manipulation. Although Vision--Language--Action (VLA) models enable end-to-end mapping from visual observations and language instructions to robot actions, adapting them to novel robot embodiments remains challenging.

This project investigates the adaptation of the $\pi$0.5 Vision--Language--Action model to a Baxter robot in a custom MuJoCo environment. A demonstration collection pipeline, LoRA-based fine-tuning framework, and real-time inference system were developed to evaluate multiple policy variants. Experimental findings show that transitioning from velocity-control to position-control improved manipulation performance and highlighted the importance of train--inference consistency during VLA adaptation.

A Vision--Language Model (VLM) planner generates manipulation sequences from current and goal workspace configurations. As planner development was presented in the Early Stage Gateway report, this report focuses on the development and evaluation of the fine-tuned VLA policy.

Project updates, demonstrations, and source code are available at \texttt{https://saniya2912.github.io/pi05-baxter/}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Literature Review}

Human--robot collaboration (HRC) aims to enable humans and robots to work together by leveraging their complementary capabilities \cite{ajoudani2018progress}. The emergence of collaborative robots has enabled safe operation in shared workspaces without physical barriers \cite{robotics8040100}. While these systems have improved physical interaction, many still rely on separate perception, planning, and control modules, limiting their ability to adapt to changing task goals.

Recent work has addressed different aspects of collaborative reasoning. SAGE \cite{kuang2025sage} estimates human actions and gaze, Therblig-based methods \cite{reinhardt2024therblig} represent manipulation as structured primitives, and long-horizon planning approaches \cite{Chen_2025} decompose complex tasks into executable action sequences. Visual instruction understanding has also been demonstrated using LEGO assembly benchmarks \cite{wang2022translating,walsman2022break}. Although effective, these methods typically depend on handcrafted task representations or specialised pipelines, limiting generalisation.

Prior to emergence of Vision--Language--Action (VLA) models, systems such as SayCan \cite{ahn2022icanisay} combined large language models with predefined robotic skills for language-guided planning. While effective for structured tasks, they relied on manually designed skill libraries rather than end-to-end visuomotor learning.

Recent VLA models instead learn a direct mapping from visual observations and language instructions to robot actions. RT-1 \cite{brohan2023rt1roboticstransformerrealworld} demonstrated large-scale robotic learning, while RT-2 \cite{brohan2023rt2visionlanguageactionmodelstransfer} incorporated vision--language pretraining to improve semantic reasoning. Open-source models such as OpenVLA \cite{kim2024openvlaopensourcevisionlanguageactionmodel} and SmolVLA \cite{shukor2025smolvlavisionlanguageactionmodelaffordable} have made VLA research more accessible, while the state-of-the-art $\pi$0 \cite{black2026pi0visionlanguageactionflowmodel} and $\pi$0.5 \cite{intelligence2025pi05visionlanguageactionmodelopenworld} models introduce flow matching to predict chunks of future actions, improving continuous manipulation. However, most VLA research focuses on policy learning rather than adaptation to new robot embodiments or integration with high-level planning.

Natural language has also become an important interface for human--robot interaction. Wang \textit{et al.} \cite{wang2026bidirectionalhumanrobotcommunicationphysical} proposed a framework for modifying robot trajectories through language, while VLA-bot \cite{WANG2026103268} combines multimodal perception with manipulation for collaborative assembly tasks. However, neither investigates efficient adaptation of large pretrained VLA models to novel robotic platforms.

Overall, recent advances in multimodal robotics have significantly improved robot perception, planning, and manipulation. Vision--Language--Action models show particular promise as general-purpose robotic policies, motivating further investigation into their adaptation and integration within collaborative robotic systems.

\section{Research Gap}

Despite recent advances in Vision--Language--Action (VLA) models, three key challenges remain. First, efficient adaptation study of pretrained VLA models to novel robot embodiments with different kinematics, camera viewpoints, and control interfaces remains underexplored. Second, high-level task planning and low-level manipulation are typically addressed separately, with few hierarchical frameworks integrating Vision--Language Models (VLMs) and VLA policies. Finally, limited attention has been given to the engineering decisions that influence VLA adaptation, including action representation, demonstration generation, and train--inference consistency.

This research addresses these challenges by adapting the $\pi$0.5 Vision--Language--Action model to a Baxter robot, evaluating key engineering decisions during fine-tuning, and integrating the resulting manipulation policy with a Vision--Language planner for goal-directed task execution.

\subsection{Research Questions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item How effectively can a pretrained Vision--Language--Action model be adapted to a novel robot embodiment?

    \item How do engineering decisions, including action representation, demonstration generation, and train--inference consistency, affect manipulation performance?

    \item Can a Vision--Language Model be integrated with a fine-tuned Vision--Language--Action policy to achieve goal-directed manipulation?
\end{enumerate}

The proposed framework (Fig.~\ref{fig:system_architecture}) follows a hierarchical architecture consisting of a Vision--Language Model (VLM) planner and a Vision--Language--Action (VLA) policy.

The Gemma-3-12B VLM compares the current and goal workspace images to generate an ordered sequence of manipulation primitives. Each primitive is provided as a language instruction to a fine-tuned $\pi$0.5 policy, which generates robot actions from visual observations and robot state information. The actions are executed within a MuJoCo simulation environment. Separating planning from execution enables independent evaluation of each component and supports future replanning when the workspace changes.

\subsubsection{$\pi$0.5}

The $\pi$0.5 \cite{intelligence2025pi05visionlanguageactionmodelopenworld} Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model released by Physical Intelligence was selected as the foundation policy for this research. The model combines visual observations, robot state information, and natural language instructions to generate robot actions directly from high-level task descriptions.

Several factors motivated its selection. First, $\pi$0.5 has demonstrated strong manipulation performance across multiple robotic embodiments. Second, the release includes a training and inference framework that can be adapted to custom robotic platforms. Finally, the model supports parameter-efficient fine-tuning, making experimentation feasible within the computational resources available for this project.

Compared to other open VLA models such as OpenVLA, $\pi$0.5 employs a flow-matching architecture that predicts action chunks rather than individual actions. This enables more expressive trajectory modelling and was considered particularly suitable for the sequential manipulation tasks investigated in this work. \subsection{Vision-Language-Action Adaptation Strategy}
\subsubsection{Need for Fine-Tuning}

Although Vision--Language--Action (VLA) models are pretrained on diverse robots and manipulation tasks, direct deployment on a new robot is challenging due to differences in kinematics, camera placement, workspace configuration, and control interfaces. The Baxter platform and structured block-manipulation tasks introduced in Section \ref{task_design} differ substantially from the original $\pi$0.5 training data, making fine-tuning necessary. The objective is therefore to adapt the pretrained policy to the target robot and task using a relatively small demonstration dataset rather than training a policy from scratch.

\subsubsection{Parameter-Efficient Adaptation using LoRA}

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) was used to fine-tune the pretrained $\pi$0.5 model. Instead of updating all 3 billion parameters, LoRA freezes the pretrained model and trains low-rank adapters within the attention layers.

Adapters were applied to both the 2B-parameter PaliGemma vision-language backbone and the 300M-parameter action expert. Using a rank of 16 resulted in approximately 30--50 million trainable parameters and reduced GPU memory usage to around 17~GB, enabling training on a single RTX 5090 GPU.

LoRA enabled efficient evaluation of multiple datasets, action representations, and state configurations while preserving the pretrained model's visual and language capabilities.

\subsection{Development of the Demonstration Collection Pipeline}
A demonstration collection pipeline was developed to generate task-specific data for fine-tuning the $\pi$0.5 model. Demonstrations were generated through scripted execution in MuJoCo, providing repeatable and scalable data collection. The pipeline consisted of four stages: task design, inverse kinematics trajectory generation, demonstration recording, and conversion to the LeRobot dataset format.

\subsubsection{Task Design}
\label{task_design}

The target task is to transform an initial block arrangement into a user-specified goal configuration. To simplify learning, the problem was decomposed into six reusable manipulation primitives involving three coloured blocks placed on a tabletop divided into near and far regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:robot_tabletop}).

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{images/goal.png}
    \caption{Tabletop workspace for execution of primitive tasks}
    \label{fig:robot_tabletop}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
\item Move the red block to the near side
\item Move the red block to the far side
\item Move the blue block to the near side
\item Move the blue block to the far side
\item Move the green block to the near side
\item Move the green block to the far side
\end{itemize}

These primitives can be combined by the VLM planner to perform more complex rearrangement tasks.


\subsubsection{Damped Least Squares Inverse Kinematics}

To generate demonstrations, a controller capable of reliably executing pick-and-place trajectories was required.

For a desired end-effector displacement $\Delta x$, the corresponding joint update $\Delta q$ is computed as

\begin{equation}
\Delta q = J^T (JJ^T + \lambda^2 I)^{-1}\Delta x
\end{equation}

where $J$ is the manipulator Jacobian and $\lambda$ is the damping coefficient.

DLS inverse kinematics was selected for its numerical stability near singular configurations and its compatibility with both simulation and future deployment on the physical robot.

% An additional advantage of the DLS formulation is its compatibility with both simulation and physical deployment. The same inverse kinematics framework can be transferred to the physical robot for future demonstration collection, reducing the amount of redevelopment required during later project stages.

\subsubsection{Scripted Demonstration Collection}

Demonstrations were generated autonomously through a scripted pick-and-place controller implemented within the MuJoCo simulation environment.

Each episode followed a structured sequence consisting of:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Move to a pre-grasp position above the target block.
\item Descend towards the block.
\item Close the gripper and establish grasp contact.
\item Lift the object above the workspace.
\item Transport the object to the target region.
\item Lower the object and release the grasp.
\item Return to a neutral configuration.
\end{enumerate}

Scripted execution ensured consistent demonstrations and enabled rapid dataset generation without teleoperation. To improve generalisation, block positions were randomised at the start of each episode while maintaining task feasibility. A total of 600 demonstrations were collected, with 100 demonstrations for each primitive task.


\subsubsection{Dataset Processing}
Recorded demonstrations were converted to the LeRobot dataset format required by the $\pi$0.5 training framework. Each sample contained workspace and wrist camera images, robot state information, a language instruction, and the corresponding action target. Dataset validation verified trajectory integrity and observation–action consistency before fine-tuning.

\subsection{Design of the Action Representation}
Two action representations were evaluated. Initial experiments used the velocity-control formulation of the original $\pi$0.5 framework, in which the policy predicts joint velocities. This was later replaced with position control, where the policy predicts target joint positions and a proportional controller generates the corresponding robot motions. The transition required new demonstrations with future joint configurations as action targets. Its impact on manipulation performance is evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:findings}.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning and Inference Pipeline}

The collected demonstrations were used to fine-tune the pretrained $\pi$0.5 model using the OpenPI training framework and datasets formatted according to the LeRobot specification.

\subsubsection{Training Configuration}

Each training sample consisted of workspace and wrist camera images, robot state information, and a language instruction. The model was trained to predict the corresponding action sequence using LoRA adapters, while the pretrained backbone remained frozen. Multiple training runs were performed to evaluate different action representations, datasets, and state configurations, with checkpoints saved every 10,000 training steps.

\subsubsection{Inference Architecture}

A two-process inference framework was developed consisting of a GPU-based policy server and a MuJoCo simulation client communicating via WebSocket. At each timestep, the client sends camera observations, robot state information, and the task instruction to the policy server. The server applies the same preprocessing used during training, performs inference with the fine-tuned $\pi$0.5 model, and returns an action chunk for execution.

For position-control policies, predicted joint positions are executed by a proportional controller within MuJoCo. This framework provides a consistent environment for checkpoint evaluation and policy comparison.

\subsection{Vision-Language Planner}

A Vision-Language Model (VLM) was introduced to provide high-level task planning within the proposed human-robot collaboration framework. While the fine-tuned $\pi$0.5 policy executes individual manipulation primitives, it has no knowledge of the desired final scene configuration. The VLM planner addresses this limitation by converting a goal scene into an ordered sequence of manipulation primitives (Fig.~\ref{fig:VLM_planner}).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{images/VLM_planner.png}
\caption{High-level VLM planning pipeline.}
\label{fig:VLM_planner}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Planner Implementation}

Gemma-3-12B was selected due to its multimodal reasoning capabilities and ability to jointly process images and text. The planner receives the current workspace image together with a goal-state image and identifies the differences between the two scenes. Prompt engineering constrains the model to output only the manipulation primitives listed in Table~\ref{tab:planner_primitives}.

To improve reliability, each coloured block is evaluated independently rather than reasoning about all objects simultaneously. For every mismatch between the current and goal scene, the planner generates the corresponding manipulation primitive. These primitives are then passed sequentially to the fine-tuned $\pi$0.5 policy as natural language instructions.

\subsection{Evaluation Methodology}

The final objective of this research is to develop a hierarchical human-robot collaboration system capable of transforming an initial workspace into a user-specified goal configuration. Since this capability depends on multiple intermediate components, evaluation was performed at three levels: primitive skill success, manipulation success, and collaborative task success.

\subsubsection{Primitive Skill Success}

Primitive skill success evaluates whether the policy performs the requested task. A trial is considered successful if the specified coloured block reaches the correct side of the workspace following the language instruction. This metric measures task understanding without considering the quality of manipulation.

\subsubsection{Manipulation Success}

Manipulation success evaluates execution quality. A successful trial requires the robot to complete the full pick-and-place sequence:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Approach the target object.
\item Grasp the object.
\item Lift the object.
\item Transport it to the target region.
\item Release the object.
\end{enumerate}

Unlike primitive skill success, this metric excludes accidental task completion caused by pushing or unintended collisions.

\subsubsection{Collaborative Task Success}
\label{sec:collab_metric}
Collaborative task success represents the final evaluation objective of the project. Starting from an initial workspace configuration, the complete planner-policy system must rearrange all objects to match a user-specified goal configuration. Success therefore depends on correct task planning and successful execution of every manipulation primitive.

Evaluation of dynamic human intervention and replanning will be completed during the final stage of the project.

Comparative evaluation was performed through rollout analysis and task success assessment to determine the impact of each engineering decision. Particular emphasis was placed on comparing velocity-control and position-control policies, with the resulting findings presented in Section~\ref{sec:findings}.

\section{Execution and Design Engineering Application}

The project followed an iterative engineering design process in which experimental findings continuously informed subsequent system development. Rather than implementing the complete collaborative framework in a single stage, individual components were developed, evaluated, and refined before integration.

Key engineering contributions included the development of a custom MuJoCo simulation environment, a scripted Damped Least Squares inverse kinematics data collection pipeline, adaptation of the $\pi$0.5 Vision-Language-Action model through LoRA fine-tuning, redesign of the action representation from velocity to position control, and implementation of a hierarchical Vision-Language planner for task sequencing. Each component was validated independently before integration into the complete planner-policy framework.

This incremental development strategy reduced hardware risk, enabled rapid experimentation, and allowed individual design decisions to be evaluated systematically before progressing towards end-to-end human-robot collaboration.

\section{Preliminary Findings and Insights}
\label{sec:findings}
\subsection{Velocity-Control Policy Evaluation}
Initial fine-tuning experiments employed a velocity-control action representation in which the policy predicted joint velocity commands. The policy demonstrated clear language grounding by consistently approaching the correct coloured block, indicating that it had learned the relationship between visual observations, language instructions, and task objectives.

However, reliable manipulation was not achieved. The \texttt{vel\_100k} checkpoint achieved a 0\% success rate across 40 evaluation trials and failed to lift the block in any trial (Table~\ref{tab:success}). Mean gripper close steps of 762 and 940 for the red-far and red-near tasks indicate that the gripper remained open throughout most of the episode, causing the robot to push the block rather than grasp it.

These results suggested that the policy had learned \emph{what} task to perform but not \emph{how} to execute it. This motivated further investigation into train--inference mismatches and the subsequent transition to position-control actions.

Analysis indicated that the poor performance could not be explained solely by the action representation, motivating investigation of the complete training and deployment pipeline.


\subsection{Analysis of Train--Inference Mismatches}
\label{sec:latch_fix}
A significant part of the project involved identifying train--inference mismatches that limited policy performance. Four major issues were identified and resolved.

The first involved incorrect action targets during dataset generation. Actions were initially recorded as small position deltas derived from velocity commands, producing target magnitudes of approximately 0.001~rad after downsampling. Consequently, the policy generated negligible arm motion during inference (mean action magnitude $\sim$0.001~rad compared to the expected 0.05--0.3~rad). This was resolved by recording the desired joint configuration at the subsequent timestep rather than position deltas.

A second issue was a grasp alignment error. During demonstration collection, the gripper consistently stopped 2--3~cm above the block due to an overly conservative descent tolerance. Diagnostic logging showed a vertical error of 28--30~mm. Reducing the descent target to $z_\text{block}-0.010$~m with a tolerance of 0.010~m reduced the error to within 1~mm.

Two deployment-specific issues were also identified:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gripper initialisation mismatch:} After simulator reset, the gripper control signal remained partially closed (\texttt{gripper\_norm} $\approx$ 0.65), causing the policy to immediately enter carry mode. Resetting the control signal to the open position restored the expected initial state.

\item \textbf{Gripper hysteresis:} The flow-matching policy occasionally generated close--open--close commands within a single action chunk, causing dropped objects during transport. This was resolved by latching the gripper closed after a successful grasp and releasing only when a placement command was detected.
\end{itemize}

These observations demonstrate that train--inference consistency is as important as the training configuration itself. Figure~\ref{fig:state_mismatch} illustrates the gripper state before and after the initialisation fix.

\begin{figure}[t] \centering \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{images/04_state_mismatch.png} \caption{Gripper state (normalised) observed by the policy over a 150-step episode, illustrating the initialisation mismatch: without the fix (dashed), the policy perceives gripper\_norm~$\approx 0.65$ at step~0 and immediately enters carry mode. After the fix (solid), the policy correctly begins with gripper\_norm~$= 0.0$ and executes the intended approach--grasp sequence.} \label{fig:state_mismatch} \end{figure}


\subsection{Position-Control Policy Evaluation}

To address the limitations observed in velocity-control experiments, the action
representation was redesigned to predict target joint positions rather than joint
velocities.
Under this formulation, a proportional controller converts predicted targets into
executable velocity commands:
\begin{equation}
    v = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(K_P\,(q_{\mathrm{target}} - q),\;
        -v_{\max},\; v_{\max}\right)
    \label{eq:pctrl}
\end{equation}
with $K_P = 40$ and $v_{\max} = 1.5$~rad/s.
This separates the \emph{where} (learned by the policy) from the \emph{how fast}
(handled by the controller), substantially simplifying the learning problem by allowing the policy to predict desired configurations while low-level control is handled separately. In practice, smoother trajectories, more stable object approach motions, and more consistent grasp attempts.
The \texttt{pos\_200k} checkpoint achieved 20\% trial-level success
(12/60 trials) compared to 0\% for the velocity-control baseline,
with the block-lifted rate rising from 0\% to 10--30\% across tasks that
had previously shown no successful manipulation.
Position-control policies were therefore adopted as the standard representation
for all subsequent training runs.

\subsubsection{Failure Mode Analysis}

Analysis of policy rollouts identified grasp precision as the dominant remaining failure mode. Although the policy consistently selected the correct object and generated appropriate approach trajectories, small spatial errors during gripper alignment frequently prevented successful object pickup. Additional failures included premature gripper release during transport and occasional policy collapse, in which the robot remained near the pre-grasp pose throughout the episode. Refer Table \ref{tab:gripper}.

An important observation was the difference in performance between workspace regions. Tasks requiring movement towards the far side achieved higher success rates than equivalent near-side tasks, suggesting that the latter were underrepresented within the training data or more difficult to execute due to workspace geometry.

Overall, the failure analysis indicates that the policy has learned the high-level structure of the manipulation task but remains limited by execution accuracy rather than task understanding. These observations motivated the expanded demonstration dataset and additional fine-tuning experiments presented in the following section.

\subsubsection{Zero-Shot Baseline Analysis}

The \texttt{pi05\_base} checkpoint result (15\% overall success, 7/10 on T0)
warrants explicit discussion.
The original $\pi$0.5 model has no Baxter-specific fine-tuning, yet achieves
80\% block-lift rate and 100\% direction accuracy on T0.
This is consistent with $\pi$0.5's pre-training on large-scale diverse robot
manipulation data: the model's pretrained manipulation priors partially
generalise to the Baxter workspace and camera configuration.

The performance collapse on blue and green blocks (0/10 success, near-zero
lift rates) demonstrates the limits of zero-shot generalisation:
the pretrained model cannot reliably distinguish between the three block colours
from the current camera viewpoint, defaulting to the dominant pretraining
behaviour (approaching objects in the centre of the scene, which corresponds
to the red block's position).
This motivates fine-tuning as a mechanism not only for embodiment adaptation
but for task routing across multiple objects with distinct spatial locations.

\subsection{Methodological Reflection}

One of the most successful methodological decisions was the adoption of a simulation-first development strategy. Initially, simulation was viewed primarily as a convenient environment for collecting demonstrations and training policies. However, the project revealed that simulation also served as an effective debugging platform, allowing numerous train--inference mismatches to be identified and resolved before deployment on physical hardware.

The iterative development process also proved valuable. Rather than treating policy training as a one-time procedure, each generation of checkpoints was analysed in detail and used to guide subsequent modifications. The VLM planner's evaluation was conducted separately, at an earlier stage of the project. This approach enabled systematic investigation of failure modes.

A limitation of the methodology is that evaluation has primarily been conducted within simulation. Although this was appropriate for rapid experimentation, the true effectiveness of the approach can only be fully assessed following deployment on the physical Robot DE NIRO platform. Consequently, the reported results demonstrate the feasibility of the proposed approach within simulation, while validation on the physical Robot DE NIRO platform remains future work.

\subsection{Lessons Learned}

The most important lesson from this project is that successful adaptation of Vision-Language-Action models depends more on system design than optimisation alone. Checkpoint comparisons showed that improvements in manipulation performance were driven primarily by changes to the action representation and improvements in train--inference consistency rather than by longer training or lower loss values.

Finally, decomposing the task into six reusable manipulation primitives simplified both policy learning and planner integration. This modular design enables more complex collaborative behaviours to be achieved through task sequencing rather than retraining the manipulation policy.

\subsection{Human-Robot Collaboration Evaluation}

The final dissertation will evaluate the complete planner--policy framework on collaborative rearrangement tasks. Given an initial and goal workspace configuration, the Vision--Language planner will generate a sequence of manipulation primitives which will then be executed by the fine-tuned $\pi$0.5 policy. Performance will be measured using the collaborative task success metric defined in Section \ref{sec:collab_metric}.

Additional experiments will evaluate dynamic replanning by introducing human modifications to the workspace during execution. The planner will be required to detect the updated scene, generate a revised task sequence, and continue progressing towards the desired goal configuration.

Thus, the remaining project timeline is structured around four major milestones:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Refinement and evaluation of the position-control manipulation policy.
\item End-to-end validation of the planner--policy architecture.
\item Deployment and testing on the physical Robot DE NIRO platform.
\item Dissertation writing, analysis, and final submission.

\end{enumerate}
The final dissertation will present the complete hierarchical framework, evaluate adaptation of the $\pi$0.5 model to a novel robot embodiment, and assess the effectiveness of integrating Vision--Language planning with Vision--Language--Action manipulation.

\section{Conclusion}

This report has presented the progress made towards developing a hierarchical human–robot collaboration framework that combines high-level Vision–Language planning with low-level Vision–Language–Action manipulation. It first motivated the need for efficient adaptation of foundation-scale VLA models to novel robotic embodiments before describing the overall system architecture, demonstration collection pipeline, and adaptation of the $\pi$0.5 model to a Baxter robot in a custom MuJoCo environment.

The report then described the development of the fine-tuning and inference framework, including the transition from velocity-control to position-control actions, the implementation of a Vision–Language planner for task sequencing, and the evaluation methodology used to assess policy performance. Experimental results and checkpoint comparisons were presented to analyse the impact of key engineering decisions, identify train–inference mismatches, and investigate the principal failure modes affecting manipulation performance.

Finally, the report reflected on the progress achieved since the Early Stage Gateway, discussed the remaining technical challenges, and outlined the plan for completing planner–policy integration, physical robot deployment, and end-to-end evaluation. Together, these developments establish the foundation for the final stage of the project, which will focus on validating the complete hierarchical framework for collaborative manipulation on a novel robotic platform.
